Framing essay

The relationship between design, technology, and the systems around us. · 1147 words · cites 6 entries from this collection

As designers, we spend a lot of time thinking about how people interact with the things we create. We think about how to make products easier to use, how to guide people through an experience, and how design can influence the way someone understands or interacts with something. But I think we sometimes overlook how much the relationship goes both ways. We design the tools people use based on how they may interact with them, but those tools also shape how people think, behave, and make decisions. The more we rely on them, the more they start to influence the way we work and even the way we approach design itself. So who is really designing whom?

In Are We Human? Notes on an Archaeology of Design, Beatriz Colomina and Mark Wigley write that “Design always presents itself as serving the human but its real ambition is to redesign the human.” No. 01 · Colomina & Wigley I found this idea interesting because it challenges the assumption that design simply responds to existing needs. Human behavior is influenced by the environments we move through, the information we interact with, and the habits we develop. The things we create become part of those environments and can gradually change how we do things. Something might be designed to make a task easier, but over time it can change how people approach it. Design does not just solve problems; it also shapes what people come to expect.

We can see this happening right now with artificial intelligence. As AI becomes a bigger part of our everyday workflows, it is not just helping us create things faster. It is also changing how we approach the creative process and what we consider valuable as designers. In her Week 6 reflection, Charitaa considers how the idea of “good taste” is changing alongside AI. She writes that it may become “less about having a good visual eye and more about knowing what is actually building and what problems are worth solving.” No. 04 · Charitaa When a tool can generate visual directions in seconds, making decisions and understanding why something should exist become even more important. But there is a difference between using a tool to support the process and letting it take over. In her Week 9 response, Valerie writes, “The opportunity for growth was robbed from you.” No. 12 · Valerie This made me think about how much learning comes from uncertainty, mistakes, and figuring things out ourselves. AI can make parts of the process faster, but relying on it for every decision can mean missing the thinking that helps us develop as designers.

But our tools are not created in a vacuum. They are shaped by the companies, markets, economies, and cultures that surround them. What gets designed is often influenced by what is profitable, what attracts investment, and what companies believe people want. These priorities can be very different from what people actually need. In her Week 5 response, Charitaa refers to a distinction Juliette made between “design for sale and design for use.” She mentions Adobe and Instagram to show how adding features does not always make a product better. She also points out that Instagram has “lost its essence in designing itself for sale.” No. 09 · Charitaa This shows how the goals behind a product can change the experience of using it. Andrea raises a related point in her Week 8 response, asking whether experiences are designed to help users or benefit a company. She brings up how Muse has a friendly AI mascot that makes people more comfortable sharing personal information. No. 13 · Andrea Something that feels welcoming can also encourage people to share more than they otherwise would. Design decisions are shaped by business models, incentives, and the goals of the people creating the product.

This raises a bigger question about the responsibility designers have in shaping people’s experiences. If design can influence behavior, we have to think beyond whether something looks good or works as intended. Crystal explores this in her Week 1 response, pointing out that the same design skills used to create something helpful can also be used to “influence, manipulate, or potentially harm people depending on the intention behind them.” She gives the example of how a missing or misplaced button could prevent someone from accessing a potentially life-changing tool. No. 10 · Crystal Connecting this to healthcare, I’ve seen how the way information is communicated can affect whether someone understands their options, feels comfortable asking questions, or knows what to do next. It made me realize that usability is not always just about convenience - it can determine whether someone can access the help they need. Being a responsible designer means considering what our decisions might make difficult, what they might encourage, and who could be left out.

At the same time, I don’t think design should only be approached through rules or a checklist of responsibilities. There is still room for curiosity, experimentation, and expression. In Designing Design, Kenya Hara describes design as “the energetic acknowledgement of our own living world through the making of things and through communication.” No. 03 · Hara We are not creating in isolation. We are responding to the world around us, while also contributing to what that world becomes. I like this perspective because it brings design back to the relationship between people and the world around them. Design is not only about producing something functional or commercially successful. It is also a way of observing, interpreting, and responding to everyday life. The reading Why A.I. Isn’t Going to Make Art raises a related question about AI and authorship, pointing out that when an AI generates a design or story from a prompt, it must make all the choices that the person prompting it did not make. No. 04 · Chiang A tool can generate an outcome based on a prompt, but the person using it still has to decide what matters, what feels meaningful, and what should be made. The creative process is shaped by our tools, but also by the judgment and intention we bring to them.

I found myself returning to this idea in one of my own responses during Week 1, when we discussed the influence designers have on the future. I wrote that “the usability of [a] product can influence the decisions and therefore influence the future of an individual person.” I also reflected on how a design decision made for one target audience can affect a much larger group of people and eventually ripple out to the wider population. No. 10 · My note Those effects can extend into larger systems and shape the expectations, biases and mental models that people have. I think this is what makes the question of who is designing whom so important. Design is never just about the object, interface, or experience in front of us. It is also about the behaviors it encourages, the systems it supports, and the people it affects. We shape the tools around us, but those tools continue to shape us in return.
